What is Pharmacology?

Let’s start with the definition. Pharmacology is the study of substances (like medicines) that interact with living systems—that includes you!

These substances work through chemical processes. Think of it like a lock and key. 🔑 The medicine (the key) binds to regulatory molecules in your body (the lock) to either activate a process or stop a process from happening.

Simply put: Pharmacology is the science of drugs. 🧪

Now, there is a specific type called Clinical Pharmacology. This is the science of substances used to prevent, diagnose, and treat diseases. It’s the practical side used by doctors to help patients.

But, we have to be careful! There is a branch called Toxicology. ☠️

Toxicology deals with the undesirable effects of chemicals on living systems. It looks at everything from individual cells up to complex ecosystems. It is the study of the poisonous effects of drugs and other chemicals. Toxicologists focus on detecting, preventing, and treating poisoning.

Chemotherapy and Drugs

Okay, let’s now move on to two very important words: Chemotherapy and Drug.

You might have heard the word Chemotherapy before. It actually means the treatment of a systemic infection or malignancy (like cancer) with specific drugs.

The goal of chemotherapy is selective toxicity. This means the drug is designed to be toxic (deadly) to the infecting organism or the bad cancer cells, but has no or minimal effects on the host cells—that’s your healthy body cells! 🛡️ We want to kill the bad guys without hurting the city.

So, what exactly is a Drug? 🤔

A drug is any substance that can bring about a change in the biological function of the body through its chemical action.

Drugs come in different forms:
🪨 Solid: Like a Paracetamol tablet or Ampicillin capsules.
💧 Liquid: Like Propranolol or Formaldehyde.
💨 Gaseous: Like the gas used in inhalers or anesthesia.

We can divide drugs into two main teams: Pharmacodynamic agents and Chemotherapeutic agents.

Pharmacokinetics vs. Pharmacodynamics

Next, we’ll look at two big words that sound similar but are very different. Imagine a drug entering your body. It goes on a journey, and it also does a job.

1️⃣ Pharmacokinetics (Movement): This involves what the body does to the drug. How does your body absorb it, move it around, and eventually get rid of it?

2️⃣ Pharmacodynamics (Power): This involves what the drug does to the body. Does it lower your fever? Does it stop the pain? That’s the power of the drug! 💪

How Drugs Interact with Body Systems

Now that we understand what drugs are, let’s talk about how they actually work inside you.

Most of the time, a drug molecule interacts with a specific molecule in your biological system called a Receptor. Remember the lock and key analogy? 🔑🔒

For a drug (the key) to interact chemically with its receptor (the lock), the drug molecule must be perfect. It needs the appropriate:
✅ Size
✅ Electrical charge
✅ Shape
✅ Atomic composition

Also, think about this: A drug is often taken in one place but needs to work in another. For example, if you have a headache, you swallow a pain tablet. 💊 The tablet goes into your stomach, but it needs to relieve pain in your head!

Therefore, a good drug must have the necessary properties to be transported from its route of administration (mouth) all the way to its site of action (head). 🧠

Dosage Form Design

Let’s now dive into Dosage Forms. What does that mean?

A dosage form is simply the physical form in which a drug is given to a patient. A tablet is a dosage form. A syrup is a dosage form.

We usually don’t give drugs as pure chemical substances. Instead, they are formulated into preparations. These can range from simple solutions to complex delivery systems. We add things called additives or excipients to help the drug work better or taste better. 🍬

Here are the different dosage forms you should know:
🔹 Tablets
🔹 Capsules
🔹 Suspensions
🔹 Solutions & Emulsions
🔹 Suppositories
🔹 Injections
🔹 Inhalers
🔹 Infusions

Oral Dosage: The Good and The Bad

At this point, it’s important to consider the most common way we take medicine: The Oral Dosage (taking it by mouth). Let’s look at the pros and cons.

👍 Advantages of Oral Administration:
1. It is convenient! You can do it yourself; you don’t need an expert.
2. It is not as expensive as injections to prepare.
3. It is usually cheaper to administer than things like infusions.

👎 Disadvantages of Oral Dosage Form:
1. It has a relatively slow onset of action (it takes time to work). 🐢
2. There are possibilities of irregular absorption.
3. Destruction of certain drugs by stomach enzymes. For example, insulin products are destroyed by stomach fluids, which is why diabetics often need shots instead of pills.

Also, changes in drug solubility can happen. For example, if you take Tetracycline (an antibiotic) with milk, it forms an insoluble complex with the calcium in the milk, and your body can't absorb it! 🥛🚫

We also have to think about Gastric Emptying Time. Drugs are essentially chemicals—weak acids or weak bases. Drugs which are weak acids will be largely unionized in the stomach, meaning they will be absorbed better there.

Routes of Drug Administration

Next, we’ll look at Routes of Administration. These are the locations where we place drugs so they can get into the body.

Different dosage forms are designed for different routes to make sure the treatment works best.

👄 Oral Route: Usually intended for systemic effects (affecting the whole body) resulting from drug absorption through the gastrointestinal tract.

💉 Parenteral Route: This basically means "not through the mouth/intestine." The three main ones are Subcutaneous (under the skin), Intramuscular (into the muscle), and Intravenous (into the vein). These are preferred in emergencies, when a patient is unconscious, or when they cannot swallow.

🧴 Topical Route: Drugs applied on the skin for local action. Absorption happens through sweat glands, hair follicles, and the skin layers.

🌬️ Respiratory Route: The lungs provide an excellent surface for absorption when the drug is a gas or aerosol. This is very useful for respiratory problems like asthma.

Traditional Medicine vs. Orthodox Medicine

Finally, let's explore Traditional Medicine. 🌿

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), Traditional Medicine is the sum total of knowledge and practice used in diagnosis, prevention, and elimination of physical, mental, and social imbalance. It relies on experience handed down from generation to generation.

This includes using plants, acupuncture, aromatherapy, and other methods.

What is the difference between Traditional and Orthodox (Modern) Medicine?

🏛️ Regulation: Traditional medicine is not well regulated by the government, whereas Orthodox medicine is strictly regulated.

🤝 Closeness: Traditional medicine is often seen as "closer to the people," while Orthodox medicine can feel more distant.

💰 Cost: Traditional medicine is usually cheaper.

🎓 Qualifications: Orthodox medicine requires stringent academic qualifications (lots of school!). This is not always the case with traditional medicine.

⚖️ Dosing: Dosage in Orthodox medicine is well regulated and measured precisely. In Traditional medicine, dosing is rarely standardized—mistakes can happen more easily.
